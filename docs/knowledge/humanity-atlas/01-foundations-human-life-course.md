## 1. Why the life course is the first organizing axis

Human health and functioning are not isolated snapshots. A life-course model follows the trajectory from before birth through infancy, childhood, adolescence, adulthood, ageing, dying and death. It treats biology, psychology, social conditions, environment and behavior as interacting influences that accumulate over time.

The 2025 World Health Organization life-course framework describes health trajectories as shaped by genetic, biological, psychosocial and environmental factors. It emphasizes critical or sensitive periods, transitions, cumulative exposures, resilience, continuity across life stages and a dignified death at any age.

ATLAS adopts the life course as the primary time axis for Humanity Atlas.

## 3. Cross-cutting forces that shape every life stage

ATLAS should never model human outcomes as purely biological. Every life stage can be altered by:

- genetics and epigenetic regulation
- anatomy and physiology
- microbiology and infectious exposure
- immune function
- nutrition
- physical environment
- housing and sanitation
- education
- income and wealth
- work and occupational conditions
- family and relationships
- violence and discrimination
- culture and religion
- law and political institutions
- healthcare access and quality
- technology and information environments
- climate and ecological change

The WHO describes social determinants of health as the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work and age, together with access to power, money and resources. These influences can generate very large health inequities and must be modeled as causal or contributory context rather than background decoration.

## 4. Major global threat classes

Humanity Atlas should classify threats into three high-level mortality / morbidity groups:

1. Communicable, maternal, perinatal and nutritional conditions
2. Noncommunicable diseases
3. Injuries and violence

Current global evidence shows that noncommunicable diseases dominate worldwide mortality, while infectious disease remains disproportionately important in lower-income settings. Cardiovascular disease is the leading global disease category by mortality, and injuries / violence remain especially important among younger people.

Risk must therefore be age-, geography-, sex-, income- and context-sensitive rather than based on one universal ranking.

## 5. Health state model used by ATLAS

Every condition or human problem should support the following states where applicable:

Normal / healthy state
→ susceptibility / risk
→ exposure or causal pathway
→ preclinical change
→ signs / symptoms
→ diagnostic evaluation
→ confirmed or working diagnosis
→ intervention
→ response
→ recovery / remission / adaptation
→ cure only when scientifically justified
→ recurrence / relapse / progression when relevant
→ disability / rehabilitation when relevant
→ palliative care when relevant
→ death when relevant

ATLAS must not collapse these distinct states into a single label.

## 6. Intervention hierarchy

For each problem, ATLAS should evaluate interventions in this order:

1. Remove or reduce the cause when possible.
2. Prevent exposure or disease before it occurs.
3. Detect early when early detection improves outcomes.
4. Treat the disease or injury using evidence-based interventions.
5. Rehabilitate lost function.
6. Manage chronic or incurable conditions.
7. Reduce suffering and preserve dignity when cure is not possible.

This allows ATLAS to distinguish a true cure from treatment, remission, management, rehabilitation and palliation.

## 7. Historical comparison framework

Historical content should be stored with the following fields:

- period
- prevailing model of the body / disease
- available diagnostics
- common interventions
- observed outcomes
- what was later validated
- what was superseded
- what was harmful
- why knowledge changed
- strongest current evidence

Example — infectious disease:

Ancient and medieval periods:
- multiple explanatory systems coexisted, including humoral and religious interpretations;
- direct observation and prognosis existed, but microorganisms were unknown.

Early modern period:
- anatomy and experimental physiology increasingly challenged inherited assumptions.

18th–19th centuries:
- vaccination emerged before modern immunology;
- germ theory, microbiology, sanitation and antisepsis transformed prevention and treatment.

20th century:
- antibiotics, vaccines, intensive care, imaging, laboratory diagnostics, blood banking, transplantation and modern public health sharply expanded medical capability.

21st century:
- genomics, targeted therapies, immunotherapies, advanced imaging, minimally invasive procedures, digital health and AI increasingly augment care, while antimicrobial resistance, inequity, misinformation, chronic disease and climate-related risks remain major challenges.

## 8. Historical anchor — smallpox

Smallpox is a canonical example for Humanity Atlas because it demonstrates the full sequence:

Disease burden
→ observation of immunity
→ variolation
→ vaccination
→ surveillance
→ coordinated public-health action
→ eradication

Smallpox existed for millennia. Jenner's 1796 vaccination work built on earlier observations and variolation practices. WHO launched global eradication efforts in the 20th century; the last naturally occurring case was recorded in 1977, and the World Health Assembly declared smallpox eradicated in 1980.

ATLAS classification: **eradicated human infectious disease**, not merely "treated".

## 9. Curriculum-derived knowledge map

University curricula reinforce that understanding humans requires multiple disciplines rather than medicine alone.

Representative domains:

### Biological and biomedical core
- chemistry and molecular interactions
- proteins and metabolism
- cell biology
- genetics and genomics
- evolution
- development
- physiology
- immunology
- microbiology
- neuroscience

### Population and public-health core
- epidemiology
- biostatistics
- demography
- environmental health
- social and behavioral determinants
- health policy
- management and systems
- prevention and population intervention

### Humanistic and social core
- psychology
- sociology
- anthropology
- culture
- ethics
- history
- philosophy
- law and institutions
- economics

### Quantitative / technical core
- mathematics
- statistics
- data science
- computer science
- engineering
- AI

Stanford's Human Biology curriculum explicitly combines genetics, evolution, ecology, cellular development and physiology with culture, society, policy, behavior and development. Johns Hopkins' MPH core combines epidemiology, biostatistics, social and behavioral determinants, environmental health, biological sciences, management, demography and policy. MIT introductory biology emphasizes molecular interactions, proteins, information flow, genetics, sequencing, cell biology, evolution and host defense.

ATLAS will use curricula as completeness checks, not as an authority ranking of professions.
