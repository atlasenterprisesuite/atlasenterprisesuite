# ATLAS Humanity Atlas — Research Track 04: HIV Prevention, Vaccine and Cure Strategies

Status: approved research direction; not a clinical protocol
Last reviewed: 2026-09-06
Primary owner: ATLAS Health / Health Frontiers
Knowledge owner: Knowledge Atlas

## 1. Research question

Can the mechanisms that prevent HIV from establishing productive infection in non-human hosts, together with vaccine, antibody, gene-editing and reservoir-targeting strategies, be combined into a safer and more scalable path toward HIV prevention and, separately, durable remission or cure?

This track was approved after comparing two ideas:

1. Why mosquitoes do not transmit HIV after feeding on a person with HIV.
2. Why HIV has not yet yielded to a conventional vaccine model comparable to measles.

The goal is to turn those questions into testable, evidence-graded research hypotheses without claiming that a mosquito contains a cure or that an HIV vaccine already exists.

## 2. Current verified state

As of 2026-09-06:

- There is no FDA-approved preventive HIV vaccine.
- There is no approved therapeutic HIV vaccine.
- Antiretroviral therapy can suppress HIV very effectively but does not usually eradicate latent reservoirs.
- A small number of confirmed or highly supported cure/remission cases have occurred after allogeneic hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation, generally performed for life-threatening cancers or blood disorders, not as routine HIV treatment.
- Broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs) remain a major research direction for prevention and treatment.
- 2026 studies reported important progress in inducing broadly cross-neutralizing HIV antibody responses through vaccination strategies, including work in non-human primates.

## 3. Why the measles-vaccine model does not directly transfer

The measles vaccine benefits from biological conditions that HIV does not share.

HIV presents additional problems:

- extreme genetic diversity and rapid evolution;
- immune escape;
- a heavily glycosylated and conformationally complex envelope protein;
- infection of CD4 T cells, which are central to immune coordination;
- integration of viral DNA into host-cell genomes;
- formation of long-lived latent reservoirs;
- lack of a common natural state in which the immune system reliably clears established HIV infection and leaves a simple protective blueprint to copy.

Therefore ATLAS should not model HIV vaccine research as "make a weaker HIV and copy measles." The useful transferable principle is broader: teach the immune system to recognize vulnerable viral structures before productive infection becomes established.

## 4. Prevention research tracks

### 4.1 Sequential immunization for bNAbs

Research objective:
Guide rare B-cell precursors through staged maturation until they produce antibodies capable of neutralizing diverse HIV variants.

Conceptual sequence:

precursor activation → lineage expansion → affinity maturation → breadth expansion → durable memory

Research questions:

- Which conserved Env epitopes are the best targets?
- Which immunogen sequence best shepherds B-cell maturation?
- How many doses and what intervals are required?
- Can several bNAb lineages be induced simultaneously to reduce escape?
- How durable is protection?

### 4.2 Multivalent / mosaic antigen strategies

Goal:
Expose the immune system to a broader representation of HIV diversity rather than one narrow viral sequence.

Evaluation:

- breadth of antibody response;
- T-cell response;
- cross-clade activity;
- resistance to viral escape;
- safety and manufacturability.

### 4.3 mRNA and other delivery platforms

ATLAS should treat mRNA, protein nanoparticles and viral-vector platforms as delivery technologies, not as cures by themselves.

Research questions:

- Which platform best presents native-like Env structures?
- Which platform supports repeated sequential immunization?
- What are the durability and reactogenicity profiles?

### 4.4 Passive bNAb prevention

The AMP studies showed that broadly neutralizing antibodies can prevent infection by HIV strains that are sufficiently sensitive to the administered antibody, while failing against resistant strains.

Implication:
A successful antibody-based preventive strategy may require combinations of antibodies targeting distinct conserved sites.

## 5. Cure / durable-remission research tracks

Prevention and cure must remain separate domains.

A preventive vaccine aims to stop infection before a durable reservoir forms.

A cure strategy must address cells that already contain integrated HIV.

### 5.1 Reservoir reduction

Key problem:
Latently infected cells can remain silent and survive despite suppressive antiretroviral therapy.

Research families:

- latency reversal / "shock and kill";
- durable silencing / "block and lock";
- immune-mediated clearance;
- targeted cytotoxic approaches;
- reservoir mapping and measurement;
- tissue-specific reservoir access.

### 5.2 CCR5 and host-cell resistance

CCR5 is an HIV co-receptor used by many HIV-1 strains.

Stem-cell transplantation with donors carrying CCR5Δ32/Δ32 has been associated with multiple HIV cure/remission cases, but 2026 evidence also indicates that CCR5 resistance alone does not explain every durable remission. Reservoir depletion, donor immune replacement and transplant-related immune effects may all contribute.

ATLAS research questions:

- Can host-cell resistance be recreated without transplantation?
- Can CCR5 modification be performed safely and at sufficient scale?
- What happens with CXCR4-tropic or dual-tropic virus?
- Which combination of resistant cells plus reservoir depletion is necessary?

### 5.3 Gene and cell therapies

Potential research classes:

- CCR5 editing;
- engineered T cells;
- engineered stem/progenitor cells;
- antiviral gene circuits;
- targeted excision or permanent silencing of proviral DNA.

Required safety evaluation:

- off-target editing;
- insertional effects;
- immune toxicity;
- clonal expansion risk;
- incomplete tissue distribution;
- viral escape.

### 5.4 Therapeutic vaccines

A therapeutic vaccine would be intended for people already living with HIV and would aim to improve immune control rather than prevent first infection.

Potential endpoints:

- delayed viral rebound after supervised treatment interruption;
- lower reservoir size;
- stronger HIV-specific T-cell responses;
- improved breadth of antibody response;
- reduced dependence on continuous ART, if proven safe.

No therapeutic HIV vaccine is currently approved.

## 6. Mosquito-inspired research track

Observation:
Mosquitoes do not transmit HIV biologically because HIV does not productively replicate through the mosquito transmission pathway and does not establish the salivary-gland infection required for mosquito-borne spread.

ATLAS hypothesis:
Study the non-permissive state mechanistically, but do not assume the mosquito contains a therapeutic molecule.

Questions:

- Which cellular-entry incompatibilities stop HIV in mosquito tissues?
- Which digestive or intracellular conditions destabilize HIV?
- Which innate antiviral pathways contribute?
- Are any mosquito antiviral factors active against retroviral processes relevant to human cells?
- Can a useful mechanism be recreated in human cells without toxicity?

Translation gate:
A mosquito-derived observation becomes a therapeutic candidate only after:

mechanism identification → molecular isolation → human-cell activity → selectivity → toxicity testing → reproducibility → animal evidence → phased clinical trials

## 7. Combined-system hypothesis

ATLAS should evaluate HIV control as a multi-layer problem rather than search for a single "magic bullet."

Possible future combination architecture:

1. Prevent exposure from establishing infection.
2. Neutralize diverse circulating virus.
3. Make susceptible host cells harder to infect.
4. Detect and reduce latent reservoirs.
5. Strengthen immune clearance of infected cells.
6. Prevent viral escape through multi-target pressure.

This is a research architecture, not a treatment recommendation.

## 8. Evidence-state labels

Every candidate must carry one of these labels:

- established clinical standard;
- approved prevention method;
- approved treatment;
- human clinical-trial evidence;
- early human evidence;
- animal evidence;
- in-vitro evidence;
- mechanistic hypothesis;
- historical/traditional claim;
- insufficient or conflicting evidence;
- disproven / unsafe.

ATLAS must never promote an in-vitro, animal or mechanistic result as a human cure.
