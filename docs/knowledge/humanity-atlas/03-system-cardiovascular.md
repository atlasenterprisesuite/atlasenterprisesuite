Status: research-backed foundation

## 1. Purpose

This node establishes the cardiovascular system as the first fully expanded organ-system model in Humanity Atlas.

ATLAS should represent cardiovascular knowledge through the canonical chain:

**normal structure/function → risk factors → pathophysiology → symptoms/signs → diagnosis → prevention → treatment → rehabilitation/recovery → cure status → uncertainty**

The cardiovascular system includes the heart and blood vessels and exists to maintain tissue perfusion, transport oxygen and nutrients, remove metabolic waste, distribute hormones and immune components, and support temperature and fluid regulation.

## 2. Normal structure and function

### Heart

The human heart has four chambers:
- right atrium
- right ventricle
- left atrium
- left ventricle

Valves keep blood moving in the intended direction. Blood returning from the body enters the right side of the heart, is pumped to the lungs for gas exchange, returns oxygenated to the left side, and is pumped through the systemic circulation.

### Blood vessels

Major functional vessel classes:
- arteries: carry blood away from the heart
- arterioles: major resistance vessels controlling regional flow
- capillaries: exchange gases, nutrients, water and metabolites
- venules and veins: return blood to the heart and serve as a volume reservoir

### Core regulated variables

ATLAS should connect cardiovascular physiology to:
- heart rate
- stroke volume
- cardiac output
- vascular resistance
- blood pressure
- circulating blood volume
- tissue oxygen delivery
- autonomic nervous system control
- kidney-mediated fluid and pressure regulation
- endocrine control

This system cannot be modeled in isolation. It is tightly integrated with lungs, kidneys, nervous system, endocrine system, blood, metabolism and immune function.

## 3. Major disease families

### Atherosclerotic disease
Includes:
- coronary artery disease
- myocardial infarction
- ischemic stroke
- peripheral artery disease

Core mechanism: progressive arterial plaque formation and, in acute events, plaque disruption and thrombosis may suddenly reduce or block blood flow.

### Hypertension
Persistently elevated blood pressure can damage arteries and organs over time and is a major risk factor for heart disease, stroke, kidney disease and other complications. It is often asymptomatic.

### Heart failure
A clinical syndrome in which the heart cannot pump or fill sufficiently to meet the body's needs without abnormal pressures. It may involve reduced or preserved ejection fraction and can result from ischemic heart disease, hypertension, valvular disease, cardiomyopathy and other causes.

### Arrhythmias
Abnormalities of cardiac rhythm or conduction. Some are benign, while others may cause syncope, stroke, hemodynamic collapse or sudden cardiac death.

### Valvular disease
Stenosis or regurgitation of one or more heart valves can alter pressure and volume loads and eventually produce heart failure or arrhythmias.

### Cardiomyopathies
Diseases of heart muscle, including dilated, hypertrophic and other inherited or acquired forms.

### Congenital heart disease
Structural cardiovascular abnormalities present from birth. Severity ranges from minor defects to critical disease requiring intervention early in life.

### Inflammatory, infectious and rheumatic disease
Includes myocarditis, pericarditis, endocarditis and rheumatic heart disease.

### Aortic and vascular disease
Includes aneurysm, dissection, vascular stenosis and other arterial disorders.

## 4. Risk architecture

### Modifiable risk factors

High-impact modifiable factors include:
- high blood pressure
- elevated atherogenic cholesterol, especially LDL-related risk
- tobacco exposure
- diabetes
- physical inactivity
- unhealthy dietary patterns
- overweight/obesity where clinically relevant
- harmful alcohol use
- poor control of established cardiovascular disease
- air pollution exposure at population level

### Non-modifiable or partially modifiable factors

- age
- family history
- inherited genetic variants
- biological sex and reproductive factors
- certain congenital conditions
- prior cardiovascular events

ATLAS must not present risk factors as deterministic. Risk is probabilistic and depends on combinations of exposures, genetics, age, comorbidities and treatment.

## 5. Prevention model

### Primordial prevention
Prevent emergence of major risk factors through healthy environments, nutrition, physical activity, tobacco avoidance, clean air, education and public policy.

### Primary prevention
Prevent the first cardiovascular event by assessing and managing blood pressure, lipids, diabetes, smoking, physical activity, diet and individualized global risk.

### Secondary prevention
After established cardiovascular disease or an event, reduce recurrence through evidence-based medication, lifestyle intervention, risk-factor control, follow-up and rehabilitation.

### Rehabilitation
Cardiac rehabilitation can combine supervised exercise, education, risk-factor management and psychosocial support after selected cardiovascular events or procedures.

## 6. Diagnostic framework

ATLAS should treat diagnosis as layered evidence, not a single test.

Potential components include:
- history and symptom characterization
- physical examination
- blood pressure and vital signs
- electrocardiogram
- blood biomarkers when appropriate
- lipid and glucose assessment
- echocardiography
- ambulatory rhythm monitoring
- exercise or pharmacologic stress testing
- CT / coronary CT angiography
- cardiac MRI
- invasive angiography or hemodynamic studies when indicated
- genetic testing in selected inherited disorders

The appropriate diagnostic pathway depends on symptoms, pretest probability, urgency and clinical context.

## 7. Symptoms and emergency states

Potential cardiovascular symptoms include:
- chest pain or pressure
- shortness of breath
- palpitations
- fainting or near-fainting
- unusual fatigue
- swelling of legs or abdomen
- reduced exercise tolerance
- neurologic deficits from stroke

Emergency patterns requiring urgent evaluation can include suspected heart attack, stroke, aortic dissection, unstable arrhythmia, cardiogenic shock and sudden severe shortness of breath.

ATLAS must never interpret a symptom list as a diagnosis. Emergency guidance must remain conservative and jurisdiction-aware.

## 8. Treatment classes

Treatment depends on the condition and can include:
- lifestyle and risk-factor modification
- antihypertensive therapy
- lipid-lowering therapy
- antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy when indicated
- antianginal treatment
- antiarrhythmic treatment
- heart-failure guideline-directed therapy
- diabetes treatment with cardiovascular benefit where appropriate
- antibiotics for selected infections
- catheter-based interventions
- coronary revascularization
- valve repair/replacement
- device therapy such as pacemakers or defibrillators
- surgery
- transplantation in selected end-stage disease

ATLAS should store treatment indication, contraindications, expected benefit, major risks, evidence grade and guideline provenance.

## 9. Cure-state model

Cardiovascular disease is not one disease and should never receive one global cure label.

Examples of ATLAS truth states:
- **preventable/reducible risk**: many atherosclerotic and hypertensive outcomes
- **treatable/manageable**: hypertension, many arrhythmias, heart failure, coronary disease
- **potentially corrected structurally**: selected congenital or valvular defects after successful intervention
- **potentially eradicated cause**: selected infectious causes when the infection is eliminated, though residual damage may remain
- **remission/control, not cure**: atherosclerosis and many chronic cardiometabolic conditions
- **advanced replacement therapy**: transplantation can replace failing organ function but creates lifelong immunologic and treatment obligations

The cure label must be condition-specific and evidence-backed.

## 10. Historical comparison

### Antiquity to early modern period
Human societies recognized pulse, chest pain, fainting and sudden death long before modern cardiovascular physiology was understood. Explanations were often embedded in humoral or spiritual frameworks.

### 17th century
William Harvey's demonstration of systemic blood circulation was a major conceptual shift toward mechanistic cardiovascular physiology.

### 18th–19th centuries
Anatomical pathology, blood-pressure measurement, auscultation and advances in physiology progressively connected symptoms to structural and functional disease.

### 20th century
Electrocardiography, cardiac catheterization, coronary care units, defibrillation, cardiopulmonary bypass, coronary bypass surgery, modern antihypertensive treatment, lipid science, thrombolysis, PCI and implantable devices transformed cardiovascular survival.

### 21st century
Risk prediction, high-resolution imaging, transcatheter interventions, inherited-disease genetics, advanced heart-failure therapies, wearable rhythm monitoring and increasingly personalized prevention continue to expand care.

Historical therapies must be labeled by evidence and period; old practices should never be promoted merely because they were historically used.

## 11. Global importance

The World Health Organization identifies cardiovascular diseases as the leading cause of death globally. WHO estimated about 19.8 million cardiovascular deaths in 2022, with heart attack and stroke accounting for most of those deaths.

This makes cardiovascular disease a high-priority Humanity Atlas domain for prevention, early detection, health literacy and systems design.

## 16. Guardrails

- Do not diagnose cardiovascular disease from a symptom alone.
- Do not report a patient's cardiovascular status without connected, current data.
- Do not use a generic "cured" label for chronic cardiovascular disease.
- Do not recommend prescription therapy without appropriate clinical context and safety review.
- Preserve guideline version, publication date and jurisdiction.
- Distinguish population-level prevention from individualized medical care.
- Escalate red-flag symptoms conservatively.
