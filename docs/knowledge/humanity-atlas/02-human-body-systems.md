Status: research-backed foundation

Secondary owner: ATLAS Health / Health Frontiers

## 1. Organizing principle

ATLAS models the human body as a hierarchy:

chemical level → cells → tissues → organs → organ systems → whole organism.

Anatomy describes structure; physiology describes function. The body depends on homeostasis: coordinated regulation that keeps key internal variables within viable ranges despite internal and external change.

For every body system, ATLAS should store the same analytical sequence:

**normal structure/function → stressor or failure mode → signs/symptoms → diagnosis → prevention → treatment → recovery/rehabilitation → cure when supported → residual risk / uncertainty.**

No organ system should be treated as isolated. Cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, endocrine, nervous, immune, digestive, musculoskeletal and integumentary processes interact continuously.

## 3. Cross-system layers ATLAS must not omit

### Blood and hematology
Blood is not merely a transport fluid. It carries gases, nutrients, hormones, immune cells, clotting components and waste products.

Knowledge branches:
- red blood cells and oxygen transport
- white blood cells and immunity
- platelets and coagulation
- plasma proteins
- anemias
- clotting and bleeding disorders
- hematologic cancers

### Genetics and molecular biology
Genetic variation influences development, metabolism, disease susceptibility and response to treatment, but genes are not destiny. ATLAS must distinguish:
- inherited pathogenic variants
- common risk variants
- somatic mutations
- epigenetic regulation
- gene–environment interaction
- uncertain variants

### Microbiome
Humans coexist with large microbial ecosystems, especially in the gut, skin, mouth and other mucosal sites. ATLAS should track microbiome evidence as an interacting biological layer, not as a universal explanation for disease.

Important caution:
- association does not prove causation;
- "dysbiosis" is often context-dependent;
- many microbiome therapies remain investigational.

### Homeostasis
Homeostasis is a system-wide concept. Temperature, blood gases, glucose, pH, blood pressure, electrolytes and fluid volume are regulated through cooperation among multiple organ systems.

This means a disease in one system can destabilize several others. Examples:
- kidney failure can alter electrolytes, acid-base balance, blood pressure and red-cell production;
- lung failure can alter oxygen, carbon dioxide and pH;
- endocrine disorders can affect cardiovascular, renal, reproductive and neurologic function.

## 5. Cure-state vocabulary

ATLAS must use controlled language:

- **preventable** — risk can be substantially reduced before disease develops
- **treatable** — interventions can improve symptoms, function or outcomes
- **controllable / manageable** — long-term control may be possible without eradication
- **remission** — disease activity is reduced or absent for a defined period
- **curable** — accepted evidence supports durable elimination of disease in a meaningful proportion of appropriately treated patients
- **eradicated** — used at a population/pathogen level when transmission has been eliminated globally, not for an individual patient
- **experimental** — intervention not established as standard care
- **insufficient evidence** — current evidence does not justify a therapeutic claim
